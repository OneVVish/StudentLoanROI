A Pell Grant, a Direct loan and a work-study job all come with a condition that keeps running after the money arrives. Every college that takes federal aid has to judge, at least once a year, whether each student is moving toward a degree fast enough and well enough. A student who falls short loses all three at once, starting with the next term.

The rule is called satisfactory academic progress, and a national study released on November 18, 2026 by Mathematica, with the Lumina Foundation, put a size on it for the first time. In spring 2025, **13.1 percent of undergraduates failed it**, about 1.8 million students. (The study's dashboard gives 1.8 million and its final report about 1.75 million.) Of those, 54 percent lost federal aid for the following term.

## What the regulation asks

The rule lives at 34 CFR 668.34. It does not set the standards itself. It tells each college what its own policy has to contain, so the details differ from school to school, but three tests appear in almost every policy.

**Grades.** The college picks a minimum GPA. For any program longer than two years, the regulation adds a floor: by the end of the second academic year the student "must have a GPA of at least a 'C' or its equivalent", or academic standing consistent with what the college requires to graduate. In the study, 96 percent of students attended a college with a 2.0 requirement at that point.

**Pace.** Credits passed divided by credits attempted, counted from the first term. The regulation lets a college measure it another way, but 91 percent of students were at colleges that set the line at 67 percent, which is two credits passed for every three attempted.

**A ceiling.** No more than 150 percent of the program's published length, "as measured in credit hours, or expressed in calendar time". On a 120-credit bachelor's degree that is 180 attempted credits, or six years. Almost every college in the study, 98 percent, used exactly that ceiling.

In the study, pace and GPA were the tests students failed most often.

## Why pace is the one that surprises people

Pace counts what was attempted, and the regulation names what lands in that count: "course incompletes, withdrawals, or repetitions, or transfers of credit from other institutions". A course dropped after the deadline is still in the denominator. So is a failed course, and so is the first attempt at a course taken twice.

Take a first semester of 15 credits. The student withdraws from one 3-credit class in October and fails another. That is 9 credits passed of 15 attempted, or 60 percent, below a 67 percent line. Nothing about the transcript says "aid" anywhere, and the term that felt like a sensible retreat is the one that did the damage.

Pace is cumulative, which cuts both ways. A second semester of 15 credits, all passed, brings the total to 24 of 30, or 80 percent, and the student is back over the line. A student who stumbles in the fourth year has far more credits behind them, so one bad term moves the figure less, and one good term moves it back less too.

Transfer credits count as both attempted and completed, so they help pace. They also count toward the 180-credit ceiling. A change of major can count too: the handbook leaves each college to decide whether credits from an earlier major stay in the total. So do remedial courses at most colleges: the regulation lets a college leave them out of the pace calculation, but 64 percent of students in the study were at colleges that counted developmental coursework toward the time limit.

## Warning, probation and appeal

Failing an evaluation does not always end aid at once. The regulation gives colleges two cushions, and a student's position depends on which ones their college uses.

A college that checks progress at the end of every term, as colleges enrolling 73 percent of students in the study did, may place a student on **financial aid warning**. That keeps aid flowing for one more term with no action needed from the student. Only about 27 percent of students who failed in spring 2025 were placed on warning.

After that comes **appeal**. Every college in the study allowed one. The regulation lists the grounds a policy names: "the death of a relative, an injury or illness of the student, or other special circumstances". The student explains why they fell short and what has changed. A college that grants an appeal places the student on **financial aid probation** for one term, often with an academic plan attached, and the student has to meet the standards or the plan by the end of it.

The study found this is where most of the aid is lost. Of every 100 students who failed nationally, 24 filed an appeal and 19 had it approved. Three in four appeals succeeded; three in four students never filed one. At two-year colleges 19 in 100 appealed, against 26 at four-year publics and 27 at four-year privates.

The colleges' own reading of why students fell short was mostly about life rather than ability. Asked what students commonly gave as the reason, colleges enrolling 92 percent of students named circumstances affecting attendance and 85 percent named health. Those are close to the regulation's own grounds for appeal.

## What a lost term costs

Losing eligibility removes every Title IV program at once: Pell, the student's own Direct loan, Parent PLUS and federal work-study. A student who stays enrolled pays for that term some other way. At the median public four-year college the in-state cost of attendance is a bit over $22,500 a year, so a semester runs about $11,300 before any state or college aid.

Leaving is not free either. A student who drops below half time starts the six-month grace period on the loans already taken, and the first payment comes due on schedule whether or not they return.

A student who has lost aid can earn it back by meeting the standards again, and the college's policy has to say how. Time alone does not do it. The Department of Education's handbook for colleges is direct about this: "paying for classes without Title IV funds or sitting out for a term does not affect a student's academic progress status, so neither is sufficient to reestablish aid eligibility." The cumulative figures have to move, which means passing courses, and outside an appeal the student pays for those courses some other way.

## What the study does not tell you

The count covers one evaluation, spring 2025. Most colleges check more than once a year, so the number of students who fail at some point in a year is larger than 1.8 million, and the study does not say by how much.

It also cannot say which policy a particular college uses. Whether it ladders its standards (26 percent of students attended a college that set a lower GPA bar in the first year), whether it warns students before they fail (68 percent did) and what an appeal requires are all in the college's own SAP policy, usually on the financial aid office's website.
