In August, Gallup asked 1,200 Americans how important a college education is. Thirty-one percent said very important, the lowest share in the seven times Gallup has asked since 1978, and less than half the 70% who said so in 2013. Another 29% said not too important. In 2013 that answer drew 6%.

A poll measures a belief, and this one cannot tell anyone whether their own degree pays. That question has an answer that depends on three things: which career the degree leads to, what it costs, and how long you are willing to wait for it. This guide runs the numbers for all three.

## What "worth it" means here

Every figure below comes from this site's calculator, run the same way for each career. The comparison is between two people at eighteen. One goes to college for four years, then works in the career, paying off whatever was borrowed. The other skips college, borrows nothing and starts working right away, at the real median pay of a high school graduate, adjusted for age. After ten years the calculator adds up what each one earned, takes the loan payments out of the graduate's side, and reports the difference.

The careers are the 177 occupations the Bureau of Labor Statistics says typically need a bachelor's degree. The wages are national medians. Taxes are left out of both sides. The whole loan is priced at 6.5% and repaid over ten years, which is the calculator's default and is generous above $27,000: past that point the money is Parent PLUS at 8.5% or private at whatever a lender charges, and the calculator's Detailed mode prices that split.

## Most degrees clear the bar, and the loan decides how many

Here is how many of the 177 finish the ten years ahead of the high school graduate, by what the degree cost and was borrowed:

| Borrowed for the degree | Careers ahead after ten years |
| --- | --- |
| Nothing | 147 of 177 |
| $27,000, the student's own federal limit | 138 of 177 |
| $50,000 | 127 of 177 |
| $92,000, the most the federal government lends, Parent PLUS included | 104 of 177 |
| $150,000 | 84 of 177 |

The first row deserves a word. It counts a degree that cost nothing in money, the way a full scholarship would, so the only price is the four years spent not earning. Thirty careers lose on that alone: the pay they lead to does not make up for starting four years late. Every row below it adds a real price, and the count falls as the price rises. At the federal maximum, close to three in five still come out ahead within the ten years.

For a sense of which row is typical: the College Board's [Trends in Student Aid 2026](https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/student-aid) finds that 47% of 2024-25 bachelor's graduates from public and private nonprofit colleges borrowed, and those who did left owing about $30,000 on average. That leaves out any Parent PLUS loan, which is in a parent's name. The $27,000 row is close to that typical borrower.

The spread underneath the table's counts is wide. At no loan, the career in the middle of the 177 finishes a bit under $178,000 ahead, and the middle half runs from about $56,000 to about $387,000. At the ends, financial managers finish a bit under $870,000 ahead and short-term substitute teachers nearly $188,000 behind. "College" in the Gallup question is all of those at once.

## Behind at ten years is usually not behind for good

Ten years is a short window for a career. The calculator also looks forty years out and finds the age at which the graduate passes the high school graduate and stays ahead. On a $27,000 loan, 172 of the 177 careers get there. The median age is 27, and a quarter of them take until 31 or later. On a $92,000 loan, 172 get there again, at a median age of 30, and a quarter not until 35.

So most of the careers that are behind after ten years are still on the way, not stuck. At either loan, five never pass the line within forty years.

How long is too long to wait is not a question the numbers can answer. A 35-year-old who is finally ahead spent seventeen years behind, from the first day of college, and ten of those years making a loan payment every month.

## What the money figures leave out on the degree's side

The calculator counts wages and nothing else. The College Board's [Education Pays 2026](https://research.collegeboard.org/trends/education-pays) tracks what comes with the wages, and some of it is money too. In 2024, 66% of bachelor's degree holders aged 25 and up working full time year-round had health insurance through their employer, against 51% of high school graduates. Retirement plans run the same direction. None of that is in the premiums above, so on this point they understate the degree.

The rest is not money at all. In the 2024 presidential election, 76% of citizens aged 25 to 44 with a bachelor's degree voted, against 42% of high school graduates the same age. In 2023, 38% of bachelor's holders volunteered, against 17%. Graduates are also more likely to exercise and less likely to smoke.

The report calls all of these associations, and the word is chosen carefully. The people who finish college differ from the people who do not in ways that shape their health and their habits too, the same problem [the rankings guide](/guides/college-roi-rankings) runs into with schools. A degree comes with these on average. Whether it causes them is a question the figures do not answer.

## What this does not count

Each of these figures assumes the student finishes the degree, finds work in the career, and earns that career's median. None of that is guaranteed, and the calculator says so rather than pricing it:

- **Not finishing.** Someone who leaves after two years carries the loan without the career. The figures above describe completers only.
- **Not starting the job.** The wages assume the first job arrives on time. [What a slow first year costs](/guides/entry-level-hiring-and-college-roi) is its own guide.
- **The school.** Every career is priced the same wherever the degree came from. Whether a more selective school adds anything is a separate question, and [the research on it](/guides/college-roi-rankings) finds less than its reputation suggests, with one important exception.
- **Your city.** National wages run below a high-cost metro's on both sides of the comparison. The calculator adjusts both when you pick a city.

## Run your own

The 177 careers above are a population. Yours is one row in it, at one price, in one city. [Put in the major or the career, the school and the loan](/?from=guide), and the calculator gives the same two answers for that one case: how far ahead or behind after ten years, and the age at which the line is crossed.
