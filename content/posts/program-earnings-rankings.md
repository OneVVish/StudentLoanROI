The New York Times published [a college-by-college guide to which majors pay off](https://www.nytimes.com/interactive/2026/10/07/your-money/college-degree-earnings-guide.html) on October 7, built by the HEA Group from earnings the federal government collects for students who received federal aid. It ranks programs rather than schools, mathematics at Duke as a separate entry from economics at Duke, and each one is the median income graduates reported to the IRS four years after finishing.

That fixes the worst problem with the older rankings, which we wrote about in [what a college ROI ranking cannot tell you](/guides/college-roi-rankings): a school-wide figure pools the engineers with the social workers and describes neither. A program figure does not.

The figures come from the Department of Education's program performance data, which this site already reads for its school search. They match. Duke mathematics is $297,029 in that file, as the Times reports, and Harvard statistics is $230,876. So the questions below are not about whether the numbers are right. They are about what a ranked list of them cannot show. Money figures from the federal file are rounded below, except figures the article prints exactly, which are quoted as published.

## Seventeen graduates

Duke's $297,029 is the median of 17 people, which the Times reports, adding that the federal earnings test itself sets aside programs smaller than 30. Harvard's statistics figure rests on 17 as well. Both sit at the top of the article's list.

A median of 17 is a real median. It is also one that two or three graduates choosing different jobs can move a long way, so the top of the list describes a handful of people at least as much as it describes a department.

The fair point in the other direction: the top of the list is not mostly small programs. Of the 100 best-paid bachelor's programs in the file, about a fifth have fewer than 30 graduates, against a third of all programs. The headline examples are thin. The list as a whole is not.

Small cohorts gather at the other end. Of 24,103 bachelor's programs with both an earnings figure and a benchmark, 284 fall short of the benchmark in the preliminary data, a bit over 1 percent. Two thirds of those 284 rest on fewer than 30 graduates, and the median one misses by about $2,400. The law already allows for that: a program loses federal loans only after failing in two years out of three, which is exactly what a rule written for noisy small samples looks like. The first measurement that counts is July 2027, so a flag in today's file is a warning and not a verdict.

## Where the graduates work

Three of the programs the Times singles out are California nursing programs: Cal State East Bay, San Francisco State and Samuel Merritt, each with graduates earning around $150,000 four years out. The article presents them as little-known names near the top of a list otherwise led by computing and math programs at schools like Caltech and Cornell.

Sort every nursing program by graduate earnings four years out, using the College Scorecard's figures for each program, and nine of the top ten are in California. The tenth is in Oregon. The median California nursing program pays its graduates about $117,000, against about $86,000 across the country and about $78,000 in Alabama.

That is mostly a fact about California, not about the nursing schools. California pays registered nurses more than any other state, a median of about $140,000 in the Bureau of Labor Statistics' state wage data, and nursing licenses are issued state by state. A school's state alone explains a little under two thirds of the difference in graduate earnings between nursing programs nationwide, with Puerto Rico left out as the Times left it out. Take California out as well and the state still explains about half.

The same calculation gives very different answers for other fields. Economics carries no state license, and there the school's state explains about 12 percent of the spread. A ranking cannot tell a nursing program that trains unusually well from one that happens to sit where nurses are paid most. The school search on this site divides nursing and law graduates' earnings by their own state's typical pay for the occupation before scoring them, for that reason, and says so beside the score.

None of this makes the $150,000 less real for a nurse who will work in the Bay Area. It makes it a poor guide for a nurse who will work in Ohio.

## Which high school graduate

The Times compares programs to "the median high school graduate", at $34,808, which is how it can say a Juilliard graduate may earn less. That is a real federal figure: it is the national benchmark the Department of Education uses for some programs.

For most programs it is not the bar. For about 85 percent of bachelor's programs the preliminary data measures graduates against high school graduates in the school's own state instead, and that bar runs from about $30,400 in Mississippi to about $41,400 in Alaska. A program can clear the national figure and miss its state's, or the reverse.

Juilliard happens to be one of the programs graded against the national figure. Its music graduates, 49 of them, report a median of $32,842, and the preliminary file flags the program. So the article's example holds. What a reader cannot tell from the article is that the next program over, in a different state, may face a different line entirely.

## What a program median still cannot see

Every figure in the ranking describes graduates who received federal aid, four years after finishing, as they reported income to the IRS. Some stock awards and deferred pay are not in it. Students who never finished are not in it at all.

And it describes who went there. The article itself cites research finding that many students who simply apply to a highly selective school earn about as much as its graduates wherever they end up enrolling, though for Black, Hispanic and lower-income students the school attended matters a great deal. A ranking sorted by graduate earnings sorts partly by who was admitted, and it cannot separate the school from the students it chose.

None of the figures knows your loan, your city or the career you are aiming at. The [calculator](/?from=guide) prices those: it takes the wages for your major or occupation where you plan to work, your actual borrowing, and sets the result against a high school graduate who borrowed nothing. It deliberately does not raise or lower a salary because of the school, for the selection reason above. The [school search](/?tool=schools&from=guide) shows the per-program federal figure beside each school's cost, as information and with the same caveats as this page, and never feeds it into the calculation.

One last detail from the article is worth knowing if you build your own list from federal data. On October 9 the Times corrected the guide for merging schools that share a name: the Anderson University in Indiana with the one in South Carolina, and several other pairs. The federal cost file this site uses holds 67 school names belonging to more than one institution, which is why every lookup here goes by federal school ID rather than by name.
