Until July 1, 2026, a medical, dental or law student could borrow the full cost of school from the government. The first $20,500 a year came as a Direct loan and everything above it as Grad PLUS, with no total limit. Grad PLUS is gone. A professional student who starts now can borrow up to $50,000 a year in Direct Unsubsidized loans, and $200,000 in total for the whole professional degree, at 8.07% for loans made this school year. Anything past that comes from a private lender or from family.

A student who was already enrolled on June 30, 2026 and already had a Direct loan for that program keeps the old rules for the lesser of three more academic years or what is left of the program. Everyone starting in 2027 is under the new ones.

## What graduates borrowed under the old rules

The College Scorecard publishes, school by school, the median federal debt of the graduates of each professional program who borrowed. Those figures come from the years before the change, so they include Grad PLUS. They show what the typical borrower at each school needed, which is the useful thing to set against the new limit.

| Program | Schools reporting | Typical school's median debt | Schools whose median is above $200,000 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Medicine | 143 | ~$193,000 | 62 |
| Dentistry | 60 | ~$253,000 | 47 |
| Law | 178 | ~$106,000 | 4 |

Law sits well under the limit almost everywhere. Medicine straddles it: at the median medical school graduates borrowed a bit under $193,000, and at 62 of the 143 schools the typical borrower needed more than $200,000. Dentistry is the program the change reaches hardest. At 47 of the 60 dental schools the median borrower was past the line, and at the median school by about $53,000.

The spread inside each program is as wide as the gap between them. Medical school medians run from about $48,000 to about $330,000, and dental school medians from about $123,000 to about $439,000. Private schools run higher in both: the typical private dental school's median is about $358,000, against about $237,000 at a public one. The school matters as much as the program.

## The money above $200,000

The part above the limit is a private loan, and it is a different kind of debt. A private lender sets the rate from the borrower's credit or a cosigner's, and a private loan carries none of the federal income-driven repayment or forgiveness. During residency the federal payment can follow the stipend; the private payment follows whatever the lender's contract says, and lenders' terms for the training years differ.

The calculator splits each path that way. When no school is picked it uses a national figure for each degree, taken from the medical, dental and law associations' own surveys rather than from the Scorecard, so it does not match the table above. With those figures and a $27,000 undergraduate loan:

| Path | Professional-school debt | Federal | Private |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Family medicine physician | $205,000 | $200,000 | $5,000 |
| General dentist | $279,900 | $200,000 | $79,900 |
| Lawyer | $130,000 | $130,000 | none |

A dentist who borrows the dental association's figure now has close to $80,000 of private money to find. At a dental school three-quarters of the way up the range, the private share passes $110,000.

## When each path comes out ahead

Professional school runs late. A physician spends four years in medical school earning nothing and three more in residency on a stipend, so the comparison that matters is not who is ahead after ten years but the age at which the graduate passes a debt-free high school graduate and stays there. With the debts above and the school years counted:

| Path | Ahead for good at age, on RAP | On Tiered Standard |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Family medicine physician | 32 | 33 |
| General dentist | 31 | 32 |
| Lawyer | 30 | 30 |

All three get there, and the repayment plan moves the age by a year at most. What the plan changes is the total. The Repayment Assistance Plan sizes the federal payment from income and forgives what is left after 30 years of payments. For the physician above, that means a bit under $620,000 of interest over the life of the loans and about $197,000 forgiven at the end. Tiered Standard pays the whole federal balance off over 25 years once school ends, with about $460,000 of interest. Neither plan touches the private part. A borrower comparing the two should know which loan each is talking about.

## What this does not count

- **Scholarships and service programs.** Anything that pays for school instead of lending for it shrinks the loan before any of this applies. The calculator takes the debt you expect, not the average.
- **Specialty pay.** The physician above is in family medicine. A specialty that pays more catches up sooner, and one with a longer residency later.
- **Public Service Loan Forgiveness.** RAP payments count toward it, and the [repayment tool](/?tool=repayment&from=guide) models the ten-year clock for someone working for a nonprofit or government employer.
- **Living costs.** The debt figures include what graduates borrowed to live on. The school-by-school prices in the [graduate school search](/?tool=gradschools&from=guide) are tuition and fees only.
